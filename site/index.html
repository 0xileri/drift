01
The insight

Attention and money move at different speeds

A token can get loud before real volume shows up — an early signal, or a paid pump. It can also move serious volume while almost nobody is talking about it, which is what quiet accumulation looks like from the outside.

Neither number tells you much alone. A social score is a popularity contest; a volume chart is a crowd you can't see the face of. The information lives in the disagreement between them, and that is the only thing Drift-d reports.

Most research agents take one data source, score it, and have a model narrate the score. Drift-d does a different job: it reads two independent signals and explains what it means when they stop agreeing.

02
Pipeline
5 stages

How a reading is produced

  1. Social momentum

    Interactions and sentiment per hourly bucket, aggregated across X, Reddit, YouTube, TikTok and web3-native chatter.

    LunarCrush time-series

  2. Onchain momentum

    Volume for the token's own Base pool, hour by hour — pool-specific, not aggregate cross-venue volume that would blur where the trading actually happened.

    GeckoTerminal OHLCV

  3. Divergence engine

    Both series are converted to momentum, scored against the token's own recent behaviour, and subtracted. Fully deterministic — no model involved at this stage.

    Local · log-ratio + robust z-score

  4. Onchain verification

    When a gap crosses the threshold, one direct call reads the pool contract's live reserves and checks them against what the aggregator reported.

    Base RPC · getReserves()

  5. Narration

    Claude reads the score and every input behind it, then explains which kind of gap it is and what to watch next. It never computes the number.

    Claude
03
The engine

Two decisions that make the number trustworthy

The scoring engine is the part worth scrutinising, because everything downstream inherits its mistakes. Two problems showed up when it was first run against real pools, and both would have produced confident, wrong output rather than an error.

Momentum is a log ratio, not a percent change

Percent change is unbounded as the previous value approaches zero. On a thin pool, one quiet hour followed by an active one produced z-scores as high as 178,000 — that is division by almost nothing, not momentum. And thin pools are exactly what this agent is built to watch, so the artifact lived precisely where the product does.

Before: max |z| 178,497 · median 0.08

After: median 0.75 · p99 7.16 · the ±10 cap binds on 0.2% of hours

But bounding an artifact is not removing it

The log ratio stopped the blow-ups without fixing what caused them. A ratio between two tiny numbers is still noise wearing a plausible score, and 62% of the first 92 events had an hour under $1,000 — one of them scored 6.17 on volume going from $3,953 to a single cent.

There is now a floor, set relative to each pool's own median rather than in dollars. Median hourly volume runs $43,214 for AERO and $347 for BNKR, so a flat cut would have dropped 3% of MORPHO's hours and 68% of KEYCAT's — deleting exactly the thin-liquidity tokens this is built to watch.

The two axes are still not on the same scale

Median/MAD equalises the middle of both distributions but not the tails. Raw p99 |z| runs 6.46 on the social side against 2.92 onchain, and an alert threshold placed in that gap is far easier for one axis to reach than the other. Dividing each axis by its own upper-decile move fixes the part it aims at: scaled p90 is 1.10 against 0.92.

It does not fix the tails, and the measured numbers say so plainly. Scaled p99 is still 2.73 against 1.76, the social axis crosses the threshold on 1.3% of hours against 0.1% for onchain, and 79% of events remain social-driven. Normalising at one quantile only matches two distributions at that quantile — social momentum has genuinely fatter tails than pool volume.

A rank transform would settle it, replacing each score with its percentile inside its own baseline so the two match at every quantile rather than one. That changes the score's units and needs recalibration, so it is named here rather than half-done and claimed.

The threshold is a rate target, not an accuracy measurement

Replayed across 4,458 real scored hours on eight tokens, a cutoff of 2.5 produces about three events a day, which is readable. That is the entire justification. Nothing about it establishes that events above the line are more often right — and the scorecard below shows they are not.

What actually happened next

The agent used to publish readings and never check them. Every explanation ended with "watch the next few intervals" and nobody watched. The hourly series was already on disk, so the test cost nothing — it just had never been run.

The claim being tested is the narrow one this agent implies: after the two series come apart, the lagging one moves. Measured on levels, not on scores — a z-score falls after a spike by construction, so comparing scores would report a fade for almost any event regardless of what the market did.

“Followed” means the lagging series averaged at least 25% above its own pre-event baseline in the hours afterwards. Threshold 2.5, 8 tokens, replayed from recorded history. Regenerate with npm run scorecard.

Read that honestly: flagged hours do worse than hours picked at random, and the gap does not close at any threshold between 1.5 and 4.0. On this month of data, across these tokens, the score does not identify hours where money is more likely to follow attention.

The engine still measures what it says it measures, and the divergences are real disagreements between two real series. What is not established is that they lead anywhere. Publishing that is the point — a claim that cannot fail is not a claim, and the scorecard is the part of this project that can.

The most likely defect is on the input side rather than in the scoring: attention is measured globally while volume is measured in a single Base pool, so the two describe different markets. Scoping both sides to the same one is the next thing to try.

05
Verification

Every figure traces to a real call

Drift-d reads market data through an aggregator, which is fast and reliable but is still somebody's summary of the chain. So when an event fires, the agent goes to the source: one direct getReserves() call reads the pool contract's live reserves and compares them against the reported liquidity.

That check is deliberately the same move as the product itself — catching two sources disagreeing — applied one layer deeper. Three of the five watched pools are V2-style pairs that support it; the other two are concentrated-liquidity pools where the call does not exist, and the agent reports that rather than pretending otherwise.

Output is committed to a public file on every run, so the record is timestamped and append-only. You can check what the agent said and when, instead of taking a demo's word for it.

Operation

Running continuously

A scheduled job polls every hour, rebuilding its baseline from the provider APIs rather than trusting cached state — a stale cache could only ever feed the engine an older picture than the source. A delayed or missed run therefore costs nothing and repairs itself on the next pass.

Readings are keyed to the hourly bucket they describe, not to the clock time they were taken, so repeated runs cannot double-count an hour. Narration is deduplicated the same way: an hour that has already been explained is never explained twice.
